VHS How the tape actually works

Slide 1 of 3 · The tape

It isn’t film. There isn’t a single picture on it.

  1. A reel of film is a stack of tiny photographs: hold it up to a lamp and you can see the movie. Hold VHS tape up to the same lamp and all you see is flat, rusty brown. The picture is stored as invisible magnetism.

    pictures on a VHS tape
    0
    JVC launches VHS in Japan
    1976

  2. The cassette is a sealed little machine: two reels, spring brakes that lock them, a dust flap, and a clear plastic leader at each end. A lamp inside the VCR rises into the cassette. When the clear leader comes by, the light shines straight through it and the machine knows it has reached the end.

    of tape in a T‑120 (2 h in SP)
    246 m
    tape width: half an inch
    12.65 mm
    cassette width (103 deep, 25 thick)
    187 mm

  3. The tape is a polyester ribbon, a fifth as thick as printer paper, with one side coated in billions of iron-oxide needles held in glue. Each needle is a tiny bar magnet. On blank tape they point every which way, and played back that sounds like hiss and looks like snow.

    total tape thickness (paper ≈ 100 µm)
    ≈20 µm
    length of one oxide needle
    ≈0.3 µm

  4. The video head is a C-shaped ferrite electromagnet with a gap narrower than a wavelength of light. Current in its coil pushes a magnetic field out through the gap, and every needle that passes under it gets flipped to match. The video signal ends up as a row of north–south stripes.

    head gap
    ≈0.3 µm
    shortest stripe (a red blood cell is ≈7 µm)
    ≈1.3 µm

  5. Playback runs the same head over the stripes. A coil only makes a voltage when the magnetism through it changes (Faraday’s law), so the head picks up the boundaries between stripes. Brightness is coded in how close together they are: frequency modulation, just like FM radio.

    FM band for black → white (NTSC)
    3.4–4.4 MHz

  6. Brush recorded tape with magnetic developer fluid and the hidden recording shows up. It still isn’t pictures. You get thousands of thin diagonal stripes, plus a band along each edge. Why diagonal? Slide 2 explains.

    diagonal stripes on a 2‑hour movie (NTSC SP)
    431,568

Slide 2 of 3 · The VCR

The tape barely moves. The heads do the running.

  1. A TV picture holds over a hundred times more signal than audio. To record it, the head has to cross the tape at almost 6 metres per second. Pull tape past a fixed head that fast and a 2‑hour cassette is over in 42 seconds.

    head-to-tape speed needed
    5.8 m/s
    T‑120 run time at that speed
    0:42
    real VHS tape speed (SP), slower than an audio cassette
    33 mm/s

  2. Press Play and guide posts pull the tape out of the cassette and wrap it around a slanted metal drum. That’s the whirr-and-clunk. The top half of the drum spins with two heads on opposite sides. The tape wraps just over halfway around, so one head is always touching it.

    drum speed (1,500 in PAL)
    1,800 rpm
    speed the heads hit the tape
    ≈21 km/h
    drum diameter
    62 mm

  3. The drum is tipped about 6° relative to the tape, and a machined ledge on the lower drum guides the tape around it in a spiral. Each pass of a head cuts one long diagonal line from the bottom edge of the tape to the top. This is helical scan.

    drum tilt
    ≈6°
    length of each track, on tape 12.65 mm wide
    ≈97 mm

  4. Each pass records one field: every other line of one TV picture, in 1/60 of a second. Head A lays a stripe, then head B lays the next one right beside it. Along the edges, fixed heads write a plain audio track on top and a control track on the bottom.

    stripes per second (NTSC)
    59.94
    ground the heads cover in 2 hours, on 246 m of tape
    ≈42 km

  5. The stripes touch, with no blank space between them. So how does head A avoid picking up head B’s stripe next door? The gaps in the two heads are tilted +6° and −6°. A head reading a stripe written at the other angle gets almost nothing. This is azimuth recording.

    track width in SP (a hair is ≈70 µm)
    58 µm
    track width in EP, the 6‑hour mode
    19 µm

Slide 3 of 3 · Tracking

Hitting a 58-micron stripe at 21 km/h

  1. Film has sprocket holes. VHS has a control track: one magnetic pulse per frame along the bottom edge. During playback a servo compares those pulses with the drum’s position and adjusts the capstan until each head arrives just as its stripe does.

    control pulses per second (25 in PAL)
    30

  2. Play a tape recorded on another VCR and its stripes sit a few microns away from where your deck expects them. The head wanders over the boundary into the neighbouring stripe, which was written at the wrong angle, so the signal drops out: that’s the bar of snow. The tracking control shifts the timing until the bar rolls away. Drag the slider under the TV to try it.

    half a track: the worst possible error
    ±29 µm

  3. The two heads take turns, so playback switches from head A to head B 60 times a second. The handoff happens just above the bottom edge of the picture, which CRT TVs hid under the bezel. Digital captures show the whole frame, which is why VHS rips have a ragged, wobbling strip along the bottom.

    before vertical sync, where the switch lands
    ≈6 lines
    decks added heads for a clean pause and slow motion
    4‑head

  4. There isn’t room for broadcast-quality colour, so VHS records brightness as FM up high and squeezes colour into a narrow band underneath it, at 629 kHz. Colour ends up with far less detail than brightness, so reds bleed sideways and edges go soft.

    horizontal resolution (DVD ≈ 500)
    ≈240 lines
    “colour-under” frequency
    629 kHz

  5. VHS Hi-Fi (1984) added two more heads to the drum. They write stereo sound as FM deep into the coating, and the video heads then write over the surface. Lower frequencies reach deeper, so both survive in the same strip of tape, one layer on top of the other. Be kind, rewind.

    Hz: Hi-Fi frequency response
    20–20k
    Hi-Fi head azimuth
    ±30°
    last VCR made (Funai, July)
    2016
